All at Once

The world rarely searches for the same thing, and when it does, it happens in four ways.

Data: Google Trends via GoogleTrendArchive · 18 months, Nov. 27, 2024 – May 17, 2026 · 125 countries

By Belen ReyesDec. 10, 2026

The morning Pope Francis died

Just before 7:50 a.m. Greenwich time on April 21, 2025, the Vatican announced that Pope Francis had died. When Google next refreshed its lists of what was trending, he was already on them in 22 countries.

Ten minutes later it was 67.

Within the hour, 114.

Moments like this are rare. Reading 18 months of trending searches, from November 27, 2024, to May 17, 2026, we found that the world looks up together in four ways:

  • Shocks: news nobody saw coming
  • Appointments: events everybody had in the calendar
  • Waves: stories that keep coming back
  • Rituals: small habits and ceremonies repeated every day or every year

This article is about them.

First, the usual

Most searches never leave home

Pope Francis was unusual. Over 18 months, about 683,000 different topics trended somewhere in the 125 countries of the archive. Almost all of them stayed where they started.

1 · Shocks

Shocks

Some moments nobody saw coming: a death, an outage, a war. When the news is big enough, it crosses borders in minutes. We looked at the 60 widest of these shocks, each checked by hand.

1 · Four stories
Four shocks stand out: ChatGPT down and Pope Francis, tied as the widest at 123 countries (Pope Francis reached half of them in ten minutes); Diogo Jota, a slow start, then 122; and Venezuela, which kept spreading for hours.
2 · What they were
Half of the news that stopped the world was a death. A quarter was the internet breaking: 30 deaths, 15 outages, 8 moments of politics or conflict and 7 others. Tap a type to see it alone.

2 · Appointments

The world keeps a fixture list

Shocks are rare. Most of the time, when the world searches for the same thing, it is because it agreed to in advance. Nine in ten global moments were scheduled, 7,236 of 7,999, and 98 percent of those were sport.

1 · The calendar
On 481 of 486 days, at least one scheduled event trended in 15 or more countries. On a typical day there were 13.
2 · Weekends
They pile up on weekends: 24 on an average Saturday, 7 on a Monday.
3 · The whole world
On 204 days, one of them reached 100 countries or more. 199 of those were football matches. The news did that 15 times in 18 months.
4 · The exceptions
The scheduled events you might expect, the Oscars, an inauguration, the conclave, are the exceptions on this calendar. Now take football away yourself.

3 · Waves

The stories that kept coming back

Some stories never quite leave. Over 18 months, the world returned again and again to a few wars, a handful of stars and the companies behind its phones and chatbots. Each return could be slow, as a story unfolded over days, or sudden, as news broke all at once.

Moments like the strikes are the exception. Of the 851 moments outside sport that reached 50 or more countries, only about one in seven was a shock. Counted from the first countries to search, the typical one took 12 hours to reach half of them.

The widest returns were wars and crises: the Israel–Iran war, the fall of Assad, the US operation in Venezuela. Most of them came back with news nobody scheduled.

Stars came back together, in awards season. In early 2025 the Golden Globes, the Grammys and the Oscars lit up the same months; a year later, the same season brought several of them back.

4 · Rituals

The searches the world repeats

Some shared moments are not news at all. Every morning, the world checks the weather. Each country does it at its own hour, so the habit circles the planet once a day. And every year the world comes back to the same holidays, even when their dates move.

Closing

Two ways to be together

When Pope Francis died, the world searched for him in the same hour: 2:20 a.m. in Mexico, 5 p.m. in Japan, nearly 8 p.m. in New Zealand. That is one way to share a moment. The weather is the other.

1
Each dot is a country, at the hour its “weather” searches usually peak, from east (top) to west. On the world’s clock, the dots climb: the morning moves west, one time zone at a time.
2
Switch to each country’s own clock and the staircase collapses: 116 of 120 countries search the weather at 6 a.m. their time.
3
Pope Francis does the opposite. On the world’s clock, 114 of 123 countries started searching within the same hour; on local clocks they scatter from the middle of the night to the evening.

Shocks line up on the world’s clock. So do appointments: a final kicks off at one moment for everyone, even where it is 3 a.m. Rituals line up on each country’s own clock: the weather at 6 a.m., the countdown at midnight. Waves do a little of both, coming back for months, sometimes slowly, sometimes all at once.

The rule we used to find the moments the world shared was built for the world’s clock: 15 countries within an hour. In 18 months it caught the weather only once, on a May morning when 13 European countries woke up in the same hour. On a usual day, about 20 countries search the weather at 6 a.m. their time. The most repeated search in the archive was almost invisible to it.
