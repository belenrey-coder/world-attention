3 · Waves

The stories that kept coming back

Some stories never quite leave. Over 18 months, the world returned again and again to a few wars, a handful of stars and the companies behind its phones and chatbots. Each return could be slow, as a story unfolded over days, or sudden, as news broke all at once.

Searches move at the speed of the news
Two returns on the same clock. When Damascus fell after a days-long advance, searches spread over half a day. When the United States struck Iran, 50 countries were searching within the hour.
Countries already searching in the 24 hours before are left out (Syria: 14; Iran on 22 June: 1; Pope Francis: 16, after Easter). Each line counts all the story's topics.

Moments like the strikes are the exception. Of the 851 moments that reached 50 or more countries, only about one in seven was a shock. Counted from the first countries to search, the typical one took 12 hours to reach half of them.

Design note: Final (Nov. 24). Takeaway: what makes a wave is that it comes back; each return can build slowly or break at once (fair baseline: wave returns are slower than shocks but not slower than ordinary moments). Sequence: one story up close → one return of each kind on a shared clock (same encoding as the Shocks chart) → the article-level line (1 in 7 shocks; 12 hours to half) → ridgeline in scroll steps (shared time axis; shape over exact values, Wilke) → martini glass: hover, open a row, filter. Options and history on boards 4a–4c; full justification in the design-rationale doc.
